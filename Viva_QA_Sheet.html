🫀 Viva Q&A Sheet

Heart Attack Prediction — Multi-Output Regression | Viswesh Anand | Class XII

📌 Basic Concept Questions
1
What is the main objective of your project?
The objective is to predict 7 cardiovascular parameters — including stenosis percentage, artery diameter, cholesterol, blood pressure, heart rate, BMI, and a composite risk score — using 5 lifestyle inputs: age, smoking, exercise, diet, and family history. Instead of simply classifying risk as high or low, regression gives continuous medical values that are more useful for analysis.
Emphasize "7 outputs" and "regression" — these show project depth.
2
What is a heart attack? How does it relate to your project?
A heart attack (myocardial infarction) occurs when blood flow through a coronary artery is blocked, starving heart muscle of oxygen. The blockage is caused by stenosis — narrowing of the artery due to plaque buildup. My project directly models stenosis percentage and artery diameter, which are the primary physical indicators of heart attack risk.
3
Why did you choose Regression instead of Classification?
Classification gives only a binary yes/no answer. Regression gives continuous numerical values — like "stenosis is at 78%" or "risk score is 65/100" — which are far more informative for medical decision-making. A doctor needs numbers, not just a label. This makes the project medically realistic and analytically richer.
This is likely the most important question. Nail this one.
4
What is stenosis?
Stenosis is the narrowing of a blood vessel due to the buildup of fatty deposits called plaque inside the artery walls — a process called atherosclerosis. As stenosis increases, the artery diameter decreases. A stenosis above 70–80% is considered severe and significantly increases heart attack risk.
5
What are the 5 inputs and 7 outputs?
Inputs: Age, Smoking (0/1), Exercise Level (0–4), Diet Score (1–10), Family History (0/1).

Outputs: Stenosis (%), Artery Diameter (mm), Cholesterol (mg/dL), Blood Pressure (mmHg), Heart Rate (bpm), BMI, and Risk Score (0–100).
🤖 Algorithm Questions
6
What is Random Forest? Why did you choose it?
Random Forest is an ensemble learning algorithm that builds multiple decision trees on random subsets of training data and averages their predictions. I chose it because: (1) It reduces overfitting — a single decision tree memorizes data, while RF generalizes better. (2) It handles non-linear medical relationships. (3) It provides feature importance scores. (4) It works well on small-to-medium datasets.
The sentence to memorize: "Random Forest reduces overfitting through ensemble averaging."
7
What is MultiOutputRegressor?
MultiOutputRegressor is a scikit-learn wrapper that trains one regression model per output variable. In my project, it trains 7 Random Forest models internally — one for each of the 7 cardiovascular parameters — and outputs all 7 predictions from a single predict() call.
8
What is overfitting? How does Random Forest solve it?
Overfitting occurs when a model learns the training data too precisely and fails to generalize to new data. A single decision tree memorizes every detail including noise. Random Forest solves this by training many trees on random data subsets (bootstrapping) and random feature subsets, then averaging — which cancels out individual tree errors.
9
What is the difference between regression and classification?
Classification predicts a discrete label (e.g., 0 or 1, cat or dog). Regression predicts a continuous numerical value (e.g., 78.5% stenosis, risk score of 65.2). My project uses regression because medical parameters are continuous quantities — a blood pressure of 145 mmHg carries different meaning than just "high."
10
What is train-test split? What ratio did you use?
Train-test split divides the dataset into a training set (used to teach the model) and a testing set (used to evaluate it on unseen data). I used an 80/20 split: 240 rows for training and 60 rows for testing. This ensures the model is evaluated on data it has never seen before.
📊 Dataset & Evaluation Questions
11
Why did you use a synthetic dataset?
Real coronary artery data requires angiography or CT scans and is not publicly available in the required format. No open-source dataset combines artery measurements with lifestyle data together. I generated a synthetic dataset using medically accepted physiological ranges and built-in logical correlations — for example, higher smoking leads to higher stenosis, which in turn reduces artery diameter.
Do NOT say "I just randomly generated data." Say "medically calibrated synthetic data."
12
What is R² Score? What values did you get?
R² (R-squared) measures how well the model explains variance in the target variable. A score of 1.0 means perfect prediction; 0.0 means the model is no better than predicting the mean. My average R² across 7 outputs is approximately 0.70. The Risk Score output achieved the best R² of 0.80.
13
What is Mean Absolute Error (MAE)?
MAE is the average of the absolute differences between predicted and actual values. A lower MAE means more accurate predictions. For example, my Stenosis prediction has an MAE of ~8.2, meaning predictions are on average 8.2 percentage points away from actual values.
14
Which feature is most important in your model?
Based on the feature importance analysis of the Random Forest model predicting stenosis, age and smoking are the top two features. This is medically logical — older age leads to more plaque buildup, and smoking accelerates atherosclerosis. Family history is third, followed by exercise level and diet score.
15
Is your model 100% accurate? What are its limitations?
No model is 100% accurate. The main limitations are: (1) The dataset is synthetic, not from real patients. (2) Real cardiovascular risk depends on many more variables like blood glucose, smoking history duration, and medication use. (3) The model is a demonstration tool — not a clinical diagnostic device. In a real deployment, it would need validation against clinical data and regulatory approval.
🌐 Website & Tools
16
What tools and libraries did you use?
Python was the primary language. Libraries used: NumPy and Pandas for data generation and manipulation; Scikit-learn for the MultiOutputRegressor and RandomForestRegressor; Matplotlib and Seaborn for visualization. The project runs in Google Colab — a free, cloud-based Jupyter Notebook environment by Google.
17
What is Google Colab? Why did you use it?
Google Colab is a free cloud-based Python notebook environment that runs in the browser without any local setup. I chose it because it requires no installation, provides free GPU/CPU compute, allows code and explanations together in cells, and can be shared easily like a Google Doc.
18

However, before real clinical use, the model would need validation on actual patient data and regulatory clearance.
